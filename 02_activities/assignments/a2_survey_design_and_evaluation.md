This survey aims to investigate the relationship between age and music taste, 
specifically how perceptions of popular music change across different age groups 
and how individuals' own preferences shift over the course of their lifetime.

and will be written up for academic publication.

Describe your target population, sampling frame, sampling units, and observational units:

Target population: Adults aged 18 and older residing in Canada, representing a 
broad range of age cohorts.

Sampling frame: A stratified list of individuals drawn from a combination of 
university participant pools, community organization mailing lists, and online 
panel recruitment platforms, stratified by age group (18–29, 30–44, 45–59, 60+) 
to ensure proportional representation across cohorts.

Sampling units: Individual adults within each age stratum.

Observational units: Individual respondents, since the survey measures each 
person's own music preferences and perceptions.

Sampling strategy: Stratified random sampling by age group. Stratification is 
justified by the research question — age is the primary variable of interest, so 
equal representation across cohorts is necessary to make valid cross-age 
comparisons, which would not be guaranteed by simple random sampling alone.

## Part B - Survey Evaluation:

Identify and describe survey features:

1. Sample type
Stratified probability sample with a cross-sectional design, using a two-stage 
sampling approach. The first stage selects groups of telephone numbers linked to 
dwelling addresses; the second stage randomly selects one eligible person aged 15 
or older per household. A "rejective sampling" procedure was also applied: all 
identified volunteers completed a long interview, while non-volunteers were 
randomly divided into a long-interview group and a short-interview group.

2. Sample size
A field sample of approximately 50,000 units was used. Of those, around 40,000 
invitation letters for the electronic questionnaire were sent to selected 
households. A completion of approximately 24,000 questionnaires was expected. 
The exact number of completed responses is documented in the Public Use Microdata 
File (PUMF) User Guide (catalogue no. 45-25-0011).

3. Target population
All persons 15 years of age and older living in private households in the ten 
provinces of Canada. Full-time residents of institutions (those residing for more 
than six months) are excluded. The territories (Yukon, Northwest Territories, 
Nunavut) are also excluded.

4. Sampling frame
A combined frame integrating landline and cellular telephone numbers from the 
Census and various administrative sources with Statistics Canada's dwelling 
(Address Register) frame. Records on the frame are groups of one or several 
telephone numbers associated with the same address, or single telephone numbers 
where no address link could be established. The frame was stratified at the 
province and Census Metropolitan Area (CMA) level, resulting in 27 strata.

5. Survey mode(s)
Two modes were available: an electronic questionnaire (EQ) — introduced for the 
first time in 2018 — and Computer-Assisted Telephone Interviewing (CATI). 
Respondents could complete the survey in either English or French. The average 
completion time was approximately 44 minutes.

6. Timeline
Data collection ran from September 4 to December 28, 2018. The reference period 
for survey questions was the 12 months preceding the interview date. Data were 
released publicly on January 26, 2021.

7. Response rate
The exact response rate is reported in the PUMF User Guide (catalogue no. 
45-25-0011). Based on the documented field sample (~50,000 units) and the 
expected completions (~24,000), the effective response rate among contacted 
households is approximately 52–60%. Participation was voluntary.

8. Weights
Each respondent is assigned a person weight reflecting how many individuals in 
the population they represent. Weights adjust for the probability of selection, 
household size, and the rejective sampling procedure applied to non-volunteers. 
Non-response adjustments and post-stratification calibration to known population 
counts are also incorporated. Weighted estimates are required for all 
population-level inference.

9. Data processing
Processing used Statistics Canada's SSPE (Survey Solutions for Processing 
Environments) generalized system. Edits were performed automatically and manually 
at both micro and macro levels, including family relationship checks, consistency 
edits (e.g., age against birth date), and flow edits to ensure respondents 
followed the correct questionnaire path. The CATI system enforced valid code 
ranges and built-in flow edits in real time during interviews.

10. Cleaning and imputation
Imputation used a nearest-donor approach based on a score function: recipient 
records (those with missing values) were matched to the most similar donor record 
based on shared characteristics, and the donor's value was substituted. Mean 
imputation among a donor pool was used as a fallback where donor imputation was 
not feasible. The process ran in nine sequential steps covering personal income, 
formal volunteering, informal volunteering, and donation variables. Personal 
income was not directly asked in 2018; instead, it was obtained by linking 
respondent records to 2017 T1FF tax data for 81.9% of respondents, with 
imputation applied to the remaining 18.1%.

11. Sources of error
Sampling error: arises from the fact that only a subset of the population was 
surveyed; estimates carry variability that can be measured through standard errors 
and coefficients of variation.
Non-response error: both unit non-response (household or individual does not 
participate) and item non-response (individual questions left blank) are present. 
Weights partially compensate for unit non-response.
Measurement error: the introduction of the electronic questionnaire in 2018 may 
have altered how respondents interpreted and answered questions relative to 
previous telephone-only iterations. This mode effect is acknowledged but cannot 
be fully quantified.
Coverage error: full-time institutional residents and residents of the three 
territories are excluded, creating a gap between the target and surveyed 
populations.

12. Limitations and known biases
Results from the 2018 cycle are not directly comparable to previous iterations 
(2013, 2010, 2007, 2004) due to the introduction of the electronic questionnaire 
and significant questionnaire redesign. The exclusion of the territories limits 
the geographic scope of national estimates. The voluntary nature of participation 
introduces the possibility of self-selection bias — individuals with stronger 
views on giving or volunteering may be more likely to respond. The rejective 
sampling design, while improving precision for volunteer-related estimates, adds 
complexity to weighting and interpretation.
